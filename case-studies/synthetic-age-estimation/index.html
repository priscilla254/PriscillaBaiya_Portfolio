Can synthetic faces teach a model to estimate age?

A two-stage generation pipeline — Stable Diffusion for identity, AgeTransGAN for aging — built to test whether synthetic data can train facial age estimators without leaning so hard on real biometric photographs.

MSc Computer Science (Artificial Intelligence) · University of Kent · Dissertation project

Age estimation from a face looks like a straightforward regression problem until you look at the data. Public datasets are privacy-sensitive, demographically skewed, and thin at the tails — young children and older adults especially. Models trained on that mix inherit the gaps. This project asked a practical question: if we generate the missing faces, can the estimator still learn a useful mapping from appearance to age?

Two-stage synthesis Stable Diffusion for identity, AgeTransGAN for age
Privacy-first data Training faces that do not belong to real people

The problem

Supervised age estimators need labelled faces at every point on the lifespan. Collecting those photographs is expensive, ethically fraught, and almost never balanced. Celebrity-heavy corpora over- represent a narrow band of ethnicity, lighting, and age. The result is familiar: mean absolute error looks acceptable in aggregate, then collapses on the groups you care about most.

Synthetic data is an appealing workaround, but only if two things hold at once. The generated face has to look the right age, and it still has to look like the same person. Drift on either axis poisons the label.

Approach

Rather than train a single model to invent a person and age them, I split the job. First, Stable Diffusion produced a pool of synthetic identities. Then a modified AgeTransGAN aged each identity into the target groups. Only after that did an age estimator see the images.

1. Synthesise identities

Diffusion generated the base faces. Prompts and filters were used to keep outputs on-distribution as faces — frontal enough to age, varied enough not to collapse into one look — without photographing anyone.

2. Age the same person

AgeTransGAN (Hsu et al., ECCV 2022) is built for this second step: an encoder–decoder generator with a conditional discriminator and an embedded age classifier. Cycle consistency, age classification, and cross-age identity loss try to pull age and identity apart. I adapted the official model so it would accept the diffusion outputs rather than the dataset it was originally trained against.

3. Estimate age

The estimator was trained and compared under a few data regimes: real faces only, synthetic faces only, and mixed. The point was not to claim a new architecture. It was to measure whether the synthetic set was doing useful work — especially where real data is scarce.

Design constraint: age had to be controllable; identity had to survive the edit. If either failed, the synthetic label was noise.

What I looked at

Evaluation sat on two layers. Image quality and identity preservation asked whether the generator was doing its job. Age error asked whether the estimator actually learned from the result.

  • Age fidelity — do transformed faces land in the intended age group, checked by an independent estimator and by inspection.
  • Identity preservation — does a face recogniser still match the aged image to its source identity.
  • Downstream error — mean absolute error and cumulative score on held-out real faces, broken down by age band.
MAE · CS@k Standard age-estimation metrics, reported overall and by band
Identity match A check that aging did not quietly swap the person

What the results showed

The useful story was in the breakdown, not the headline number. Synthetic data was strongest as a complement: it thickened the sparse age bands that real datasets under-represent. Used alone, it was more brittle — generators leave texture artefacts, and an estimator can learn those instead of aging.

Identity drift showed up most on the largest age jumps (child to adult, adult to elderly). Those were exactly the transformations the pipeline was meant to unlock, and exactly where the losses had to work hardest. Mixing a modest amount of real data with the synthetic set was the more stable training recipe.

Trade-offs

Synthetic data does not remove bias; it relocates it. Diffusion models and aging GANs both carry the demographics of their training sets. If those generators under-represent a group, the estimator will too — with the extra risk that the failure looks like a clean, balanced dataset.

There is also a domain gap. A model can do well on synthetic faces and still stumble on real cameras, compression, and pose. That is why every claim in this project is anchored on real held-out images, not on the generated test set.

What I would do next

  1. Tighten identity preservation on large age gaps, or reject transformations that fail a verification threshold before they enter training.
  2. Audit the synthetic pool for demographic coverage instead of assuming generation equals diversity.
  3. Compare diffusion-native aging (editing in latent or prompt space) against the two-stage GAN pipeline, on the same estimator.
