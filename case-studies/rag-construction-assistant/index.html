RAG Construction Assistant

UK building regulations and HSE site guidance run to thousands of pages across three separate documents. Ctrl+F on a merged PDF doesn't tell you which clause actually applies to your situation — and asking a general-purpose LLM is worse, since it will confidently invent a plausible-sounding clause number rather than admit it doesn't know.

The problem

Construction questions span two very different kinds of source material: formal regulations (the Approved Documents, cited by clause number like 3.24) and HSE safety guidance (HSG141 and HSG150, cited by numbered paragraph instead) — different citation structures that a single search strategy has to reconcile. This is guidance sourced from three official documents, not legal advice.

What I built

A local RAG pipeline with a Streamlit front end, built end to end: PDF ingestion, per-document-type chunking, embedding into a vector store, hybrid keyword + semantic search, and grounded generation with inline citations. The demo includes a question box, a cited-sources panel, and a small gallery of test questions — including ones the corpus genuinely can't answer, to show the assistant declining rather than guessing. A CLI version exists alongside the web app.

Implementation, eval set, and tests are in the repo.

How it works

Each document gets its own chunking logic rather than one generic splitter — clauses for the regulations, numbered paragraphs for the HSE guides — since forcing a single strategy across genuinely different document structures was the first thing that broke. Every chunk is tagged with a short context trail (which Part, which section) before it's embedded, so a short, otherwise-meaningless clause still carries enough context to be found and cited correctly — but that trail is hidden from the user, who only ever sees clean answer text.

Retrieval combines two search methods: semantic search for paraphrased questions (matching "110V tools" to "reduced low voltage" even without shared wording), and keyword search for exact terms and clause numbers that semantic matching alone tends to blur together. An early side-by-side test made the case for combining them concretely — semantic search alone got 4 of 5 test questions right; adding keyword search got 5 of 5.

The most interesting finding came from a fix that didn't work. One document was consistently crowding out the other two in search results whenever a topic overlapped across all three — a first-aid question, for example, would return five near-identical passages from the same source and miss the more specific HSG150 guidance entirely. The obvious fix seemed to be searching each document independently and merging the results, guaranteeing every source a fair chance. It didn't help — recall actually dropped, from 0.80 to 0.75, because forcing representation from every source pulled in some genuinely weak, off-topic matches just to fill the quota. What actually worked was simpler: keep one combined ranking, but cap any single document at three of the five results, then backfill from deeper in the list if a document runs out of good matches. That one change lifted recall to 0.90 without forcing anything artificial.

Generation is restricted to only the passages retrieved for that question — every claim in the answer must carry a citation number, and the source list itself is built directly from the retrieved chunks' own metadata, so a document name can't be invented even by accident.

Results

Measured against 20 hand-labelled questions with known correct answers, plus 5 additional questions the corpus has no answer to (excluded from the accuracy score, but checked separately to confirm the assistant declines rather than guesses).

Approach Recall@5 Correct (of 20)
Combined ranking, no cap 0.80 16
Fully independent per-document search 0.75 14
Combined ranking, capped at 3 per document (shipped) 0.90 18

The two remaining misses both retrieved the correct document, just not the precisely correct paragraph within it — a narrower, more specific gap than a wrong-document failure.

What I'd do next

  1. A reranking step or tighter paragraph-level matching to close the two remaining misses.
  2. A query-rewriting step for ambiguous questions.
